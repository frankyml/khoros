# Project background

Notes collected by the people who looked after the shop over the years, kept here because some of the older
names and habits still turn up in conversations about the project. Nothing below describes how the code works;
it is the story of the place it came from, the people who ran it, and the market hall around it.

## Where the project comes from

The codebase started as a teaching example for a small independent shop that sold tea, books and stationery
from a single counter in an old market hall. The owners kept their first price list in a paper ledger, ruled in
pencil, with one column per supplier and a second ledger for returns. When the shop began taking orders by
telephone, a neighbour who taught evening classes in bookkeeping offered to turn the ledgers into a program, and
the first version was written over a winter on a borrowed laptop.

Over the years the shop changed hands twice. The second owners added a delivery round on Thursdays, which is why
older notes mention "Thursday stock" and "the van list". The third owners moved the counter to a corner unit with
a window display, introduced loyalty stamps on paper cards, and started selling a small range of ceramics made by
a studio two streets away. Some product codes still carry the studio's initials.

## Vocabulary you may meet in old notes

- **Counter price**: the price written on the shelf label, before any promotion.
- **Van list**: the list of items reserved for the Thursday delivery round.
- **Stamp card**: a paper loyalty card; ten stamps earned a free pot of tea.
- **Back room**: the small storage room behind the counter, where opened boxes were kept.
- **Studio line**: the ceramics range from the neighbouring studio, restocked once a month.
- **Market day**: Saturdays, when the hall was busiest and the shop opened an hour earlier.
- **Ledger week**: the last week of each quarter, when the paper ledgers were reconciled.
- **Window piece**: whichever item was in the window display that week.

## Seasons in the shop

Spring brought green teas and gardening books. Summer was quiet except for tourists, who mostly bought postcards
and small ceramic cups. Autumn was the busiest season for stationery, because of the school term, and for the
richer black teas. Winter meant gift sets: a book, a tin of tea and a mug, wrapped in brown paper with string.
The owners liked to say that the shop had four weathers and that each one smelled different.

## People who appear in the history

The founders were a retired schoolteacher and her brother, a former ship's cook who handled the tea blending.
The bookkeeping neighbour wrote the first program and later trained two of the shop assistants to keep it
running. A local illustrator drew the shop's logo, a teapot balanced on a stack of books, which still appears on
the paper bags. The ceramics studio is run by two potters who fire their kiln on Mondays.

## The market hall

The hall dates from the late nineteenth century. It has a glass roof supported by iron columns painted dark
green, a clock above the main entrance that runs four minutes fast, and about forty stalls arranged in three
aisles. The tea shop's first counter sat between a cheese seller and a stall that repaired watches. On market
days a brass band sometimes played near the fountain at the end of the central aisle.

## Things that have not changed

The shelf labels are still handwritten. The tea is still weighed on a balance scale for customers who ask. The
shop still closes for two weeks every August, and the sign in the window still says "gone to the seaside".
Regular customers are still offered a cup of whatever is brewing when they come in on a cold day.

## A note on names

Some product names in the catalogue come from the founders' family: a breakfast blend named after their mother,
a smoky tea named after the cook's old ship, and a notebook range named after the street where the shop first
stood. These names are kept for sentimental reasons and do not describe the products.

## Odds and ends

The shop cat, a large grey animal called Ledger, slept in the window display for eleven years and appears in
several of the illustrator's drawings. The first telephone order was for three tins of breakfast tea and a
dictionary. The longest-serving shop assistant worked there for twenty-two years and could name every regular
customer's usual order without looking it up. The ceramics studio once made a set of mugs shaped like the
market hall clock, and a few of them are still on the top shelf behind the counter, not for sale.

## The neighbouring stalls

The cheese seller to the left of the first counter came from a farming family in the hills and sold three kinds
of hard cheese, one soft cheese in summer only, and butter wrapped in waxed paper. The watch repairer to the
right worked with a loupe screwed into one eye and kept a radio tuned to a classical station, turned down low.
Across the aisle was a flower stall whose owner arrived at five in the morning on market days, and a stall that
sold second-hand records sorted by the colour of their sleeves rather than by artist.

## The delivery round

The Thursday van was a small blue vehicle with a sliding side door that stuck in cold weather. The round covered
two villages and a row of cottages by the river, and took most of the afternoon. Customers on the round usually
ordered the same things every week, so the van list rarely changed, and the driver often stopped for tea at the
last house, where an elderly couple kept bees and paid partly in honey.

## The illustrations

The illustrator who drew the logo also made a set of small drawings for the paper bags: a teapot, a stack of
books, a fountain pen, the market hall clock and, later, the cat. Each season one of the drawings was printed in
a different colour: green in spring, yellow in summer, rust in autumn and dark blue in winter. Collectors among
the regular customers kept a bag of each colour.

## Opening hours over the years

The first owners opened from nine to five, six days a week. The second owners added late opening on Fridays
until seven. The third owners kept the Friday evenings, opened an hour earlier on market days, and briefly tried
Sunday mornings one summer before deciding that Sundays belonged to the potters' kiln and to rest.

## The fountain

The fountain at the end of the central aisle has a stone basin and a bronze figure of a woman pouring water from
a jug. Children throw coins into it, and once a year the hall's caretaker empties the basin and gives the coins
to the local school for its library. The tea shop's founders used to sponsor the first new book bought each year.
